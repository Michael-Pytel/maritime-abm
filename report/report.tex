\begin{abstract}
Between 70--96\,\% of maritime casualties involve human error~\cite{imohuman2018}, and adverse weather compounds it by degrading both perception and the very communications crews rely on to coordinate avoidance. We present a tick-based maritime agent-based model on the real Baltic and North Sea, in which vessels follow hand-authored shipping tracks and resolve encounters through a Closest-Point-of-Approach (CPA) detector coupled to a COLREGs-style give-way rule. The contribution is a quantified causal chain from \emph{environmental and human degradation} to \emph{collision outcome}: the success of every collision-warning exchange is gated by the product of local weather severity and a circadian crew-fatigue scalar, so tired watch-keepers in heavy weather miss warnings and collide. Collision consequences are evaluated with an accredited probabilistic damage model (DTU SIMCOL), external collision frequency is cross-validated against the regulator-grade IWRAP Mk II model, and a search-and-rescue subsystem layers IAMSAR random-search detection (MASSIM) with month-conditioned Arctic environmental degradation (Ashrafi). Against a classical and a weather-aware baseline, the system is evaluated across four scenarios with 30 seed-matched runs per condition using Mann--Whitney U tests with Holm--Bonferroni correction and Cliff's $\delta$ effect sizes. We hypothesise that fatigue-aware watch scheduling reduces the fatal-event rate by ${\geq}20\,\%$ and improves post-incident survival by ${\geq}15\,\%$ relative to the classical baseline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% ==================================================================

Maritime transport carries roughly 80\,\% of global trade, yet the sector's safety record is dominated by a persistent human-error problem. The International Maritime Organization (IMO) attributes 70--96\,\% of all incidents to human causes~\cite{imohuman2018}, and the European Maritime Safety Agency (EMSA) identifies weather as a compounding factor in a disproportionate share of total-loss events, particularly in congested coastal corridors where crew fatigue and information latency interact~\cite{emsa2023}. Hull and machinery insurance claims exceed USD 3.5\,billion annually~\cite{iumi2023}; even a 5\,\% reduction in fatal incidents would yield hundreds of millions in avoided liability.

The International Regulations for Preventing Collisions at Sea (COLREGs)~\cite{colregs1972} provide a deterministic collision-avoidance baseline whose implicit assumption --- that crews perceive the encounter geometry accurately and communicate intentions reliably --- fails under two compounding conditions that prior agent-based maritime simulation~\cite{tam2010,silveira2013,zhang2015} rarely couples directly to outcome:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Environmental degradation of communication.} Reduced visibility, sea clutter, and heavy weather degrade the bridge-to-bridge (VHF) exchanges through which two converging vessels agree who gives way. Most collision-avoidance models assume that, once a risk is detected, the manoeuvre is always executed.

    \item \textbf{Human-factor coupling.} Crew fatigue accumulates over watch cycles and follows a circadian pattern with a late-night performance nadir~\cite{chauvin2013}. Prior models isolate fatigue in peripheral sub-models without a direct, quantified connection to whether a warning is acted upon.
\end{itemize}

The contribution of this work is a maritime agent-based model in which the success probability of every collision-warning exchange is the \emph{product} of an environmental factor and a crew-fatigue factor, creating a quantified causal link from \emph{information quality under stress} to \emph{collision and survival outcomes}. We build the model on the real Baltic and North Sea geography (Section~\ref{sec:model}), drive vessels along hand-authored shipping tracks, and resolve encounters with a CPA/TCPA detector and a COLREGs-style give-way rule (Section~\ref{sec:avoidance}). Collision consequences use the accredited DTU SIMCOL probabilistic damage model (Section~\ref{sec:consequence}); the resulting collision frequency is cross-validated against IWRAP Mk II (Section~\ref{sec:iwrap}); and a search-and-rescue subsystem (Section~\ref{sec:sar}) closes the loop from incident to survival. The engine is implemented in Rust on the krABMaga agent-based framework~\cite{krabmaga2023} for deterministic, data-parallel batch execution. Figure~\ref{fig:causal} summarises the causal pipeline that ties these components together.

\section{Research Objectives}
\label{sec:goal}
% ==================================================================

The central thesis is: \emph{coupling crew-fatigue dynamics to the reliability of collision-warning communication, and managing fatigue through smart watch scheduling, reduces fatal maritime outcomes more effectively than weather-unaware classical practice.}

\begin{description}
    \item[Experimental conditions.] All experiments compare three methods, implemented as configuration switches over a single agent codebase:
        \begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em, labelindent=0pt]
            \item[Baseline~A (Classical)] represents current weather-unaware practice: vessels follow COLREGs give-way with idealised (perfect) VHF communication, no weather-induced slowdown, and an unmanaged fatigue build-up.
            \item[Baseline~B (Shore Rest Alerts)] adds weather-aware speed reduction and shore-issued rest advisories that slow fatigue accumulation, but no further mitigation.
            \item[Proposed System] adds smart watch scheduling (lowest fatigue build-rate) on top of the weather-aware mitigations of Baseline~B. Performance is measured using six KPIs defined in Table~\ref{tab:kpis}.
        \end{description}

    \item[Falsifiable hypotheses.] They drive all design decisions; any component that contributes to none of them is excluded.
        \begin{description}[leftmargin=2.2em, labelindent=0pt]
            \item[\textbf{H1.}] Fatigue-aware scheduling (Proposed) reduces the fatal-event rate (Table~\ref{tab:kpis}, KPI~1) by ${\geq}20\,\%$ versus Baseline~A ($p < 0.05$, Mann--Whitney U, Cliff's $|\delta| \geq 0.2$).
            \item[\textbf{H2.}] Earlier, better-coordinated avoidance and a functioning rescue chain improve post-incident survival (Table~\ref{tab:kpis}, KPI~4) by ${\geq}15\,\%$ versus Baseline~A.
            \item[\textbf{H3.}] The Proposed System outperforms Baseline~B on the fatal-event rate ($p < 0.05$) under the storm-corridor scenario, where comms degradation is most severe.
        \end{description}
\end{description}

\section{System Model}
\label{sec:model}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{World Geometry and Projection}

The simulation occupies the real Baltic and North Sea basin, bounded by latitude $\phi \in [50.5^\circ, 66.0^\circ]$\,N and longitude $\lambda \in [-5.0^\circ, 31.0^\circ]$\,E --- from the English Channel and Dutch coast in the south-west to the Gulf of Finland in the east and the mid-Norwegian coast in the north. The domain is mapped with an equirectangular projection carrying a cosine-of-mid-latitude correction, so that one field unit equals exactly one nautical mile on both axes at the reference parallel $\phi_0 = \tfrac12(\phi_{\min}+\phi_{\max}) = 58.25^\circ$. With $k_{\text{lat}} = 60$\,nm/$^\circ$ and $k_{\text{lon}} = 60\cos\phi_0$, a geodetic point projects to field coordinates
%
\begin{equation}
  x = (\lambda - \lambda_{\min})\,k_{\text{lon}}, \qquad
  y = (\phi - \phi_{\min})\,k_{\text{lat}}.
  \label{eq:proj}
\end{equation}
%
The domain measures $930$\,nm north--south ($15.5^\circ \times 60$) and ${\approx}1136$\,nm east--west; the residual east--west distortion is at most ${\sim}6$--$10\,\%$ at the box edges and is accepted without correction terms, so the engine treats all distances as Euclidean nautical miles. The coastline is loaded from Natural-Earth polygons into an R-tree spatial index supporting accelerated point-in-polygon (land) and segment-intersection (grounding) tests.

\subsection{Time Resolution}

Time advances in discrete ticks of $\Delta t = 15$\,min ($0.25$\,h). The default horizon is $T = 2880$ ticks ($30$ days). A wall-clock-of-day variable, initialised at 06:00\,UTC, advances $0.25$\,h per tick and drives the circadian fatigue term (Section~\ref{sec:fatigue}). All stochastic draws use a single per-run seeded generator (\texttt{SmallRng}), so identical seeds reproduce byte-identical KPI logs.

\subsection{Agent Types and Voyage Logic}

The model contains vessel agents and, on incident, rescue agents (Section~\ref{sec:sar}). Each vessel $i$ carries position $\mathbf{p}_i$, an ordered route $R_i = (\mathbf{w}_0,\dots,\mathbf{w}_{m-1})$ of waypoints, a route index and travel direction $d_i \in \{+1,-1\}$, heading $\psi_i$, cruise speed $v_i$, a discrete state $\sigma_i \in \{\textsc{Active}, \textsc{Docked}\}$, crew count $n_i = 10$, and an internal fatigue scalar $F_i \in [0,1]$.

Routes are \emph{hand-authored} ordered keypoint polylines drawn in a browser-based editor (\texttt{tools/waypoint\_editor.html}) and stored as \texttt{data/ais\_paths.json} with schema \texttt{mmsi/name/vessel\_type/waypoints[]}. Fixing the lane geometry by hand --- rather than replaying raw AIS --- gives a controlled, reproducible traffic substrate for the information-quality experiment while preserving realistic basin-scale routing. Ports are likewise hand-authored polygons (\texttt{data/ports.json}); a vessel that enters a port polygon docks.

Cruise speed is drawn at spawn by vessel type ($u \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)$):
%
\begin{equation}
  v_i =
  \begin{cases}
  15 + 8u & \text{passenger / ferry / ro-ro / ro-pax}\\
  10 + 4u & \text{tanker}\\
  10 + 6u & \text{cargo / container / bulk}\\
  10 + 10u & \text{otherwise.}
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:speed}
\end{equation}
%
\textbf{Waypoint pursuit.} With active target $\mathbf{t}$ (an avoidance waypoint if queued, else the current route waypoint), $\boldsymbol{\delta} = \mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}_i$ and $\rho = \lVert\boldsymbol{\delta}\rVert$, a waypoint is reached when $\rho < 0.5$\,nm. The per-tick step is $s = \min(v_i\,\Delta t,\ \rho)$ and the proposed move is $\mathbf{p}_i' = \mathbf{p}_i + (\boldsymbol{\delta}/\rho)\,s$, with compass heading $\psi_i = (90^\circ - \operatorname{atan2}(\delta_y,\delta_x)) \bmod 360^\circ$. To prevent tunnelling through thin landmasses, the move is accepted at the first of up to five geometrically halved step lengths $s\cdot 2^{-j}$ ($j=0,\dots,4$) whose segment does not cross land; a post-move grounding guard reverts $\mathbf{p}_i$ to the last valid position if it ended on land. On reaching a port or route endpoint a vessel docks for a randomised dwell $D \sim \mathcal{U}\{8,48\}$ ticks, then reverses ($d_i \leftarrow -d_i$). A fixed fleet size $N$ is maintained: when $|\text{fleet}| < N$, a fresh vessel is spawned onto a random hand-authored track.

\textbf{Same-destination following.} Co-directional traffic bound for the same destination is not separated by the give-way rule (an overtaking pair has near-zero relative velocity, Section~\ref{sec:avoidance}), so a leader--follower discipline prevents vessels from drawing up side-by-side and overlapping. Two vessels share a destination when their route endpoints (in the travel direction) lie within $5$\,nm of each other. A trailing vessel within a vicinity $r_v = 3$\,nm of such a leader --- the vessel ahead, i.e.\ closer to the shared destination --- caps its speed to the leader's, so it matches speed and does not overtake; if it closes within a keep-distance gap $g = 0.5$\,nm it holds station (anchors). On first acquiring a leader it is radioed to slow down, match speed, and keep distance. This applies anywhere the courses converge, not only at the port mouth, and yields a stop-and-go queue whose throughput matches the leader's speed.

\subsection{Weather and Hazard Field}
\label{sec:weather}

A $50\times50$-cell grid evolves three physical channels --- sea state $s$, visibility $v$, and wind $u$, each in $[0,1]$ --- which are fused into a scalar hazard $W_{ij}\in[0,1]$:
%
\begin{equation}
  W = \mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{w_s\,s + w_v\,(1-v) + w_u\,u}{w_s + w_v + w_u},\,0,\,1\right),
  \label{eq:W}
\end{equation}
%
with weights $(w_s, w_v, w_u) = (1.0, 0.5, 0.8)$. A vessel samples $W$ at its current cell only (nearest-cell lookup), so each vessel acts on local conditions. The field advances each tick through seven ordered stages (full parameters in Section~\ref{sec:weather-impl}): (i)~a two-state calm/storm regime Markov chain selecting mean-reversion targets; (ii)~up to eight Lagrangian storm cells with growth, decay, drift and removal; (iii)~an AR(1)-with-advection background per channel; (iv)~a system overlay imprinting storm cells onto the channels; (v)~cross-channel coupling; (vi)~5-point smoothing with a gradient limiter; and (vii)~hazard fusion via Eq.~\ref{eq:W}.

Independently of the stochastic field, the \textbf{storm corridor} scenario carries a single moving storm zone of radius $r_{\text{storm}}$ that drifts at $0.30$\,nm/tick along a fixed lat/lon track (English Channel $\to$ North Sea $\to$ Skagerrak $\to$ Kattegat $\to$ Baltic $\to$ Gda\'nsk). Inside the zone, both communication reliability (Section~\ref{sec:avoidance}) and vessel speed (Section~\ref{sec:speed}) are degraded.

\section{Crew Fatigue Dynamics}
\label{sec:fatigue}
% ==================================================================

Each vessel carries an internal fatigue scalar $F_i \in [0,1]$ ($0$ rested, $1$ exhausted), updated every tick. The circadian drive peaks near 03:00 and troughs near 15:00:
%
\begin{equation}
  c(t_{\text{utc}}) = \tfrac12\Big(1 - \cos\big(\tfrac{2\pi (t_{\text{utc}} - 3)}{24}\big)\Big) \in [0,1].
  \label{eq:circadian}
\end{equation}
%
While \textsc{Active}, with local hazard $W$, fatigue accumulates as
%
\begin{equation}
  \Delta F = \big(\beta_{\text{base}} + \beta_W\,W + \beta_c\,c(t_{\text{utc}})\big)\,m(\text{method}),
  \label{eq:dF}
\end{equation}
%
with $(\beta_{\text{base}}, \beta_W, \beta_c) = (0.0015,\,0.0040,\,0.0008)$ and a method build-rate
%
\begin{equation}
  m(\text{method}) =
  \begin{cases}
  1.00 & \text{Baseline A (unmanaged)}\\
  0.82 & \text{Baseline B (shore rest alerts)}\\
  0.65 & \text{Proposed (smart scheduling)},
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:m}
\end{equation}
%
and $F_i \leftarrow \min(1,\,F_i + \Delta F)$. While \textsc{Docked}, the crew recovers at $F_i \leftarrow \max(0,\,F_i - 0.025)$ per tick (full recovery in ${\approx}40$ ticks). The method factor $m$ is the sole lever distinguishing the three conditions' human-factor behaviour, isolating the effect of fatigue management.

\textbf{Fatigue $\to$ communication degradation.} Tired watch-keepers miss or delay warnings. With threshold $F^\ast = 0.40$ and maximum penalty $\kappa = 0.65$,
%
\begin{equation}
  g(F_i) =
  \begin{cases}
  1, & F_i \le F^\ast\\[4pt]
  1 - \kappa\,\dfrac{F_i - F^\ast}{1 - F^\ast}, & F_i > F^\ast
  \end{cases}
  \;\in [0.35,\ 1].
  \label{eq:gF}
\end{equation}
%
This factor multiplies into the collision-warning success probability (Eq.~\ref{eq:pcomms}), the mechanism through which fatigue translates into collisions.

\section{Collision Avoidance}
\label{sec:avoidance}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{CPA / TCPA Detection}

Each \textsc{Active} pair is screened with the standard linear-motion closest-point-of-approach result. The field-frame velocity for heading $\psi$ and speed $v$ is $\mathbf{v}(\psi,v) = v\,\Delta t\,(\sin\psi,\ \cos\psi)$. For a pair $(a,b)$ with relative position $\Delta\mathbf{p} = \mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a$ and relative velocity $\Delta\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_b - \mathbf{v}_a$,
%
\begin{equation}
  t^\ast = -\,\frac{\Delta\mathbf{p}\cdot\Delta\mathbf{v}}{\lVert\Delta\mathbf{v}\rVert^2},
  \qquad
  d_{\text{CPA}} = \big\lVert \Delta\mathbf{p} + t^\ast\,\Delta\mathbf{v} \big\rVert,
  \label{eq:cpa}
\end{equation}
%
with $t^\ast$ measured in ticks. Degenerate cases return $(d_{\text{CPA}}, t^\ast) = (\lVert\Delta\mathbf{p}\rVert, \infty)$ when $\lVert\Delta\mathbf{v}\rVert^2 < 10^{-9}$ (parallel) and $(\lVert\Delta\mathbf{p}\rVert, 0)$ when $t^\ast \le 0$ (diverging). A manoeuvre is considered iff \emph{all} of
%
\begin{equation}
  \lVert\Delta\mathbf{p}\rVert \le 20\ \text{nm}, \quad
  d_{\text{CPA}} \le 5\ \text{nm}, \quad
  0 < t^\ast \le 12\ \text{ticks},
  \label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
%
hold and the give-way vessel is not in avoidance cooldown.

\subsection{Give-Way Rule and Communication Success}

The vessel with the lower identifier is the give-way (turn-to-starboard) vessel; the other stands on. A deterministic lower-id rule is a reproducible surrogate for the COLREGs crossing/head-on stand-on/give-way assignment~\cite{colregs1972,tam2010}. Crucially, the manoeuvre is executed \emph{only if the warning is successfully communicated}, with success probability the product of an environmental factor and a fatigue factor over both vessels:
%
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{comms}} = \underbrace{\min\big(q(\mathbf{p}_a),\,q(\mathbf{p}_b)\big)}_{\text{weather / storm}}
  \cdot
  \underbrace{\min\big(g(F_a),\,g(F_b)\big)}_{\text{fatigue, Eq.~\ref{eq:gF}}},
  \label{eq:pcomms}
\end{equation}
%
where $q(\mathbf{p})$ is the nominal link reliability, reduced to the storm-comms rate (as low as $0.08$ inside the storm corridor --- 92\,\% of warnings lost) when $\mathbf{p}$ lies in the storm zone. The manoeuvre proceeds iff a uniform draw is below $P_{\text{comms}}$; otherwise the give-way vessel fails to act, leaving the encounter to resolve by geometry alone. Equation~\ref{eq:pcomms} is the model's central coupling: it makes warning delivery jointly contingent on weather and human fatigue.

A give-way vessel receives one temporary waypoint placed $L_f = 3$\,nm ahead and $L_o = 5$\,nm to starboard of its heading $\psi$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:cpa}):
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{w}_{\text{avoid}} = \mathbf{p} + L_f\,(\sin\psi,\ \cos\psi) + L_o\,(\cos\psi,\ -\sin\psi).
  \label{eq:avoidwp}
\end{equation}
%
A three-message exchange is logged --- \texttt{CollisionWarning\{cpa,tta\}} $\to$ \texttt{GivingWay} $\to$ \texttt{MaintainingCourse}, with \texttt{ResumeRoute} once the avoidance waypoint is consumed. Each issued warning records its $t^\ast$ for the average-TTA KPI, and a 3-tick cooldown prevents manoeuvre thrashing.

\subsection{Optional Continuous-Avoidance Track (Force Field)}
\label{sec:forcefield}

As an optional realism layer, the discrete give-way waypoint of Eq.~\ref{eq:avoidwp} can be replaced by a continuous artificial-force-field manoeuvre in the style of Xiao et al.~\cite{xiao2013}, in which COLREGs behaviour emerges rather than being scripted. A vessel responds to a repulsive force from each nearby obstacle (vessel or shoal) within an effect distance $d_o$,
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{F}_{\text{rep}} = \sum_{k:\,d_k < d_o} \frac{k_{\text{rep}}}{d_k^{2}}\,\hat{\mathbf{n}}_k,
  \label{eq:forcefield}
\end{equation}
%
where $d_k$ is the distance to obstacle $k$, $\hat{\mathbf{n}}_k$ the unit vector away from it, and $k_{\text{rep}}$ a steepness constant. The resultant force sets a rudder angle that drives a first-order Nomoto manoeuvring response with non-dimensional indices $K' = K(L/v)$, $T' = T(v/L)$. The effect distance is calibrated from AIS encounter statistics, with $d_o \sim \mathcal{N}(1548,\,706^2)$\,m for head-on and $\mathcal{N}(384,\,358^2)$\,m for overtaking encounters~\cite{xiao2013}. In this track, the crew-preparedness state scales $k_{\text{rep}}$, so a fatigued crew yields weaker, later avoidance. % TODO-VERIFY exact repulsion exponent in Eq.~\ref{eq:forcefield} against Xiao et al. (extraction ambiguous; inverse-square assumed as the standard artificial-potential-field form).

\section{Collision Consequence Model (SIMCOL)}
\label{sec:consequence}
% ==================================================================

A \textbf{hard collision} is logged when two \textsc{Active} vessels close within $d_{\text{hit}} = 2\,r_{\text{coll}} = 0.10$\,nm (${\approx}185$\,m, roughly a ship-length sum), counted once per encounter --- on the tick the pair \emph{enters} contact, not every tick it remains overlapping. Encounters within $1$\,nm of any port are treated as VTS-controlled harbour manoeuvring and are not counted as collisions. The consequence of a hard collision is evaluated with the DTU SIMCOL probabilistic damage and survivability model~\cite{lutzen2001}, which replaces an ad-hoc casualty rule with collision mechanics.

\textbf{External dynamics.} The energy released for hull deformation is the loss of kinetic energy of the rigid-body system between first contact and the common-velocity state, evaluated with the closed-form external-dynamics solution of Pedersen and Zhang~\cite{pedersenzhang1998} as used by Lützen~\cite{lutzen2001,pedersen1995}. Only the component of the closing velocity \emph{normal} to the struck vessel's side performs side-penetrating work, so for struck and striking displacements $M_a$, $M_b$ the absorbed energy is the reduced-mass energy on that normal component,
%
\begin{equation}
  E_{\text{abs}} = \tfrac12\,\frac{M_a'\,M_b'}{M_a' + M_b'}\,V_n^2,
  \qquad
  V_n = \big|(\mathbf{v}_b - \mathbf{v}_a)\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\big|,
  \label{eq:eabs}
\end{equation}
%
where $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ is the unit normal to the struck side and $M_a' = M_a(1+m_{\text{sway}})$, $M_b' = M_b(1+m_{\text{surge}})$ carry hydrodynamic added mass with sway coefficient $m_{\text{sway}} = 0.85$ and surge coefficient $m_{\text{surge}} = 0.05$~\cite{lutzen2001}. The normal-velocity projection $V_n$ supplies the collision-angle dependence directly --- a near-parallel graze releases almost no energy while a perpendicular impact releases the maximum --- so Eq.~\ref{eq:eabs} reduces to the classical right-angle reduced-mass form when $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ aligns with the closing velocity, and no separate oblique-reduction factor is required.

\textbf{Internal mechanics.} The absorbed energy is related to damage extent through Minorsky's absorbed-energy/resistance-volume correlation~\cite{minorsky1959}, $E_{\text{abs}}\,[\text{MJ}] = 47.2\,R_T + 32.7$, where $R_T$ is the resistance (destroyed) volume of deformed structural material. Lützen casts the resulting damage as \emph{non-dimensional} distributions --- the damage length $\ell/L_{pp}$ (longitudinal extent normalised by struck-ship length) and the penetration $t/B$ (transverse extent normalised by breadth), each fitted to striking-ship displacement, speed, and collision angle~\cite{lutzen2001}. Taking a representative damage length $\ell = 0.12\,L_{pp}$ (the median of that distribution) and an effective smeared side-steel thickness $\tau = 0.08$\,m, the breach penetration is $t = R_T/(\ell\,\tau)$, expressed non-dimensionally as $t/B$.

\textbf{Survivability.} Because the engine does not carry per-ship residual-stability curves, the survivability factor $S_i \in [0,1]$ --- the conditional probability that the struck vessel survives the flooding case --- is modelled as a SOLAS~II-1-shaped surrogate~\cite{solas2020} on the relative penetration:
%
\begin{equation}
  S_i =
  \begin{cases}
    1, & t/B \le r_0\\[2pt]
    \left(\dfrac{r_1 - t/B}{r_1 - r_0}\right)^{0.25}, & r_0 < t/B < r_1\\[8pt]
    0, & t/B \ge r_1,
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:sfactor}
\end{equation}
%
with knee $r_0 = 0.10$, full-loss penetration $r_1 = 0.30$, and the $0.25$ exponent of the SOLAS~II-1 $s$-factor. The expected number of crew thrown into the water (man-overboard) per collision is then
%
\begin{equation}
  \mathbb{E}[\text{overboard}] = n_b\,\big(1 - S_i\big),
  \label{eq:overboard}
\end{equation}
%
with $n_b$ the struck-ship crew. Each crew member is an independent Bernoulli trial with overboard probability $1 - S_i$, and each colliding vessel is evaluated independently as the struck ship. This draw does \emph{not} kill directly: it determines how many crew enter the water, whose fate is decided by the search-and-rescue chain (Section~\ref{sec:sar}). The consequence then follows one of two parallel casualty paths. A vessel whose survival factor falls below the founder threshold $S^{\dagger} = 0.50$ founders, evacuating its \emph{whole} crew to a liferaft (the \textsc{Evac} chain); a struck vessel that stays afloat instead casts only its $\mathbb{E}[\text{overboard}]$ casualties into the water as individual persons-in-water for the man-overboard search (Section~\ref{sec:mob}). A fatality therefore arises only when a person in the water is not recovered within the survival window; $S_i \to 1$ is logged as a near-miss damage event. % TODO-VERIFY surrogate knee r_0, full-loss r_1 and founder threshold S-dagger are calibration values shaped to the SOLAS II-1 s-factor, not constants extracted from a source.

\textbf{Encounter geometry.} Every logged collision is additionally classified by its encounter geometry, taken from the angle between the two vessels' headings folded onto $[0^\circ,180^\circ]$, $\theta = \min(|\psi_a - \psi_b|,\,360^\circ - |\psi_a - \psi_b|)$:
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{type} =
  \begin{cases}
    \text{side-to-side (overtaking)}, & \theta < 45^\circ\\
    \text{front-to-side (crossing)},  & 45^\circ \le \theta < 135^\circ\\
    \text{head-on}, & \theta \ge 135^\circ.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:colltype}
\end{equation}
%
Per-type counts are accumulated as diagnostic KPIs (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}). The classification is consistent with the consequence chain: near-parallel side-to-side hits have $\hat{\mathbf{n}}$ nearly orthogonal to the closing velocity, so $V_n \to 0$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:eabs} and they release little energy, whereas head-on and crossing encounters carry the large normal closing speed that drives deep penetration and foundering.

\section{External Collision-Frequency Validation (IWRAP)}
\label{sec:iwrap}
% ==================================================================

The internal KPIs (Section~\ref{sec:experiment}) measure relative differences between methods but are not, by themselves, regulator-grade frequencies. To anchor the model to accepted practice, each run's vessel tracks are exported as synthetic AIS and analysed offline with the IWRAP Mk II model~\cite{friishansen2008iwrap}. The expected number of collisions on a waterway is the product of a geometric candidate count and a causation probability~\cite{lusic2015,friishansen2008iwrap}:
%
\begin{equation}
  N_c = N_a\,P_c.
  \label{eq:iwrap}
\end{equation}
%
For two opposing flows of classes $i,j$ over a leg of sailing distance $D$ and fairway width $W$ during time $t$, the head-on candidate count is
%
\begin{equation}
  N_a^{\text{head}} = \frac{D\,t}{W}\sum_{i,j} Q_i^{(1)} Q_j^{(2)}\,
  \frac{(B_i^{(1)}+B_j^{(2)})\,(V_i^{(1)}+V_j^{(2)})}{V_i^{(1)}\,V_j^{(2)}},
  \label{eq:nahead}
\end{equation}
%
with traffic flows $Q$, beams $B$, speeds $V$; the overtaking form replaces $(V_i+V_j)$ with $|V_i - V_j|$. For crossing waterways meeting at angle $\Theta \in (10^\circ,170^\circ)$,
%
\begin{equation}
  N_a^{\text{cross}} = \sum_{i,j} Q_i^{(1)} Q_j^{(2)}\,
  \frac{D_{ij}\,V_{ij}}{V_i^{(1)}\,V_j^{(2)}}\,\frac{1}{\sin\Theta},
  \label{eq:nacross}
\end{equation}
%
where $V_{ij} = \sqrt{(V_i^{(1)})^2 + (V_j^{(2)})^2 - 2 V_i^{(1)} V_j^{(2)}\cos\Theta}$ is the relative speed and $D_{ij}$ the Pedersen collision diameter~\cite{friishansen2008iwrap,pedersen1995}. The causation probabilities are the IWRAP software defaults~\cite{friishansen2008iwrap,lusic2015}: $P_c^{\text{head-on}} = 0.50\times10^{-4}$, $P_c^{\text{overtaking}} = 1.10\times10^{-4}$, and $P_c^{\text{crossing}} = 1.30\times10^{-4}$. An offline analyzer builds the leg network from the hand-authored routes, derives per-leg flows from the fleet and traversal times, and sums the head-on, overtaking, and crossing contributions into a per-year $N_c$. For the present network this yields $N_c \approx 0.01$ collisions/yr, within the regulator acceptance of ${<}1$ collision/yr/fairway; comparing $N_c$ between Baseline~A and the Proposed System (whose weather-aware slow-down lowers traffic flow) provides an externally interpretable, regulator-aligned check that the fatigue-coupling mechanism produces frequency reductions of the expected order.

\section{Search-and-Rescue Subsystem}
\label{sec:sar}
% ==================================================================

When a collision founders a vessel (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfactor}, $S_i < S^{\dagger}$) or a grounding forces an evacuation, the survivors enter a liferaft and the search-and-rescue (SAR) chain is triggered. A rescue agent is dispatched from the nearest shore base (a port) after a mobilisation delay $\Delta t_{\text{mob}} = 2$ ticks --- a helicopter ($80$\,kn) when the datum lies beyond $40$\,nm offshore, otherwise a patrol vessel ($25$\,kn)~\cite{imosar2018}. The asset then progresses through mobilise, transit, search, and embark phases; the subsystem layers two accredited components onto this transit. A second, parallel SAR chain handles the persons cast overboard from struck vessels that stay afloat (Eq.~\ref{eq:overboard}); it shares the same drift and random-search detection model but resolves each casualty individually (Section~\ref{sec:mob}). Figure~\ref{fig:fsm} shows the resulting vessel state machine.

\subsection{Drift and Random-Search Detection (MASSIM)}

Because the exact distress position is uncertain and the liferaft drifts, detection is modelled with the Koopman random-search formulation used in the MASSIM maritime-search model~\cite{karatas2018,koopman1980}. A searcher of sweep width $w$ moving at speed $v_s$ over a search area $A$ has instantaneous detection rate $\gamma(t) = w\,v_s / A$. The cumulative detection probability for $n$ searchers over elapsed search time $\tau$ is
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{CDP} = 1 - \exp\Big(-\,\frac{n\,w\,v_s\,\tau}{A}\Big) = 1 - e^{-C},
  \label{eq:cdp}
\end{equation}
%
where $C = n\,w\,v_s\,\tau / A$ is the search coverage factor and $v_s$ the asset's (season-degraded, Section~\ref{sec:sar-ashrafi}) on-scene speed. The search area expands as the datum drifts: with an initial uncertainty radius $r_0$ and drift speed $u_d$, the area grows as $A(\tau) = \pi\,(r_0 + u_d\,\tau)^2$, so detection becomes harder the longer the response is delayed --- the quantitative link from response latency to survival. Each on-scene tick advects the liferaft datum along the prevailing current and draws a Bernoulli detection at the per-tick coverage $1 - e^{-\mathrm{d}C}$~\cite{karatas2018}.

\subsection{Environmental Degradation (Ashrafi)}
\label{sec:sar-ashrafi}

Rescue-asset performance degrades with season following the Arctic SAR framework of Ashrafi et al.~\cite{ashrafi2024}, in which air temperature, wind speed, significant wave height, and a wind-chill index combine into a per-month severity. The wind-chill index uses the standard form
%
\begin{equation}
  \text{wci} = 13.12 + 0.6215\,T - 11.37\,U^{0.16} + 0.3965\,T\,U^{0.16},
  \label{eq:wci}
\end{equation}
%
with air temperature $T$ ($^\circ$C) and wind speed $U$ (km/h). Rather than re-deriving the expert-assigned metocean thresholds, we adopt the framework's published month-group aggregation: each calendar month maps to one of four severity groups --- A (May--August, mildest), B (April, September), C (March, October, November), and D (January, February, December, harshest)~\cite{ashrafi2024}. The group multiplicatively reduces the asset's transit and search speed --- helicopter $1.00/0.95/0.77/0.60$ and patrol vessel $1.00/0.80/0.40/0.21$ for groups A--D, relative to the mildest group --- and sets the per-survivor boarding time ($2.1$--$10.7$\,min by helicopter, $6.1$--$31.2$\,min by vessel)~\cite{ashrafi2024}. On location, the asset embarks the $n_b$ survivors at this season-degraded rate before the case is logged \textsc{Rescued}. Transit, search (Eq.~\ref{eq:cdp}), and boarding therefore all lengthen in winter, so --- reproducing the source case study --- the longest rescue times occur December--February and the shortest May--August~\cite{ashrafi2024}.

\subsection{Man-Overboard Search}
\label{sec:mob}

The casualties cast into the water by a struck-but-afloat vessel (Eq.~\ref{eq:overboard}) are handled as an explicit person-in-water search (Fig.~\ref{fig:mob}), following the man-overboard simulation of Karatas et al.~\cite{karatas2018}. Each collision spawns an \emph{incident}: its $\mathbb{E}[\text{overboard}]$ casualties become individual persons-in-water, scattered about the collision point and each assigned an independent drift bearing, so a search may recover some of an incident's people before others. One searcher --- selected and dispatched by the same nearest-base, helicopter-versus-patrol rule as the liferaft chain --- is tasked per incident and homes on the cluster centroid. While on scene it draws the Koopman per-tick detection of Eq.~\ref{eq:cdp} \emph{independently for every person}, recovering each as it is located. A person not recovered before the cold-water survival window $\tau_{\text{mob}} = 12$ ticks ($\approx 3$\,h) elapses is lost; this is the model's only path to a crew fatality. The window is far shorter than the $96$-tick liferaft endurance, reflecting unprotected immersion, and shrinks the effective margin in winter through the same Ashrafi speed degradation that slows the searcher (Section~\ref{sec:sar-ashrafi}). The two SAR chains thus differ only in granularity: the liferaft recovers a vessel's crew as one whole-group case, the man-overboard search resolves individuals.

%
\begin{equation}
  P_{\text{prep}} = \max(0,\,1 - \alpha_M W)\cdot\max(0,\,1 - 0.70\,F).

\end{equation}

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

For every (KPI $\times$ scenario $\times$ method-pair), the two methods are compared with a two-tailed Mann--Whitney U test (average-rank tie handling, normal approximation): with $\mu_U = n_1 n_2 / 2$, $\sigma_U = \sqrt{n_1 n_2 (n_1+n_2+1)/12}$, $z = (U - \mu_U)/\sigma_U$, and $p = 2\Phi(-|z|)$, where $\Phi$ is evaluated by the Abramowitz--Stegun 26.2.17 rational approximation. The effect size is Cliff's $\delta = (U_1 - U_2)/(n_1 n_2) \in [-1,1]$~\cite{cliff1993}. Multiplicity is controlled by Holm--Bonferroni step-down over all tests; a result is \emph{significant} if $p_{\text{corr}} < 0.05$ and \emph{confirmed} if additionally $|\delta| \ge 0.2$. Method pairs tested are (A vs Proposed), (B vs Proposed), and (A vs B). The same seed sequence is applied to all conditions, eliminating seed-selection bias from pairwise comparisons.

\section{Implementation}
\label{sec:implementation}
% ==================================================================

\subsection{Engine and Execution}

The model is implemented in Rust on the krABMaga agent-based framework~\cite{krabmaga2023}, with an axum/tokio REST + WebSocket server for live run control and a rayon-parallel batch runner; a React/Leaflet frontend renders vessels, ports, the storm zone, the weather grid, the comms log, and KPIs live. Batch runs write per-run JSONL tick logs, a manifest, and a \texttt{summary.csv} for analysis. Vessel agents are scheduled at krABMaga ordering 200 and a single post-tick agent at ordering 1000.

\subsection{Simulation Loop}
\label{sec:loop}

Each macro tick runs four ordered phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:tick}) whose ordering is a causal constraint: (1)~\emph{weather} --- advance the field and apply any scenario-scripted event; (2)~\emph{perception \& communication} --- each vessel samples local hazard, updates fatigue (Eqs.~\ref{eq:circadian}--\ref{eq:gF}), and CPA-screened encounters attempt their warning exchange (Eq.~\ref{eq:pcomms}); (3)~\emph{agent step} --- vessels navigate at hazard-gated speed (Section~\ref{sec:speed}) or hold for avoidance, and rescue agents decrement mobilisation or advance under the SAR penalty (Section~\ref{sec:sar}); (4)~\emph{physics \& housekeeping} --- hard-collision and grounding checks, SIMCOL consequence evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:consequence}), terminal-vessel respawn, logging, and clock advance.

\subsection{Speed Reduction under Hazard}
\label{sec:speed}

Two multiplicative slow-downs are applied by interpolating the just-moved position back toward the last valid position by a factor $f$. The hazard-gated factor (Baseline~B and Proposed only; Baseline~A ignores weather) is
%
\begin{equation}
  f_W = \max\big(0.30,\ 1 - 0.45\,W\big),
  \label{eq:fw}
\end{equation}
%
and a storm-zone factor $f_{\text{storm}} \in \{1.0\,(\text{A}),\,0.45\,(\text{storm corridor}),\,0.70\,(\text{default})\}$ damps speed inside the moving zone, acting as an implicit braking mechanism that lowers closing rates in deteriorating weather.

\subsection{Weather Process Parameters}
\label{sec:weather-impl}

The seven-stage field of Section~\ref{sec:weather} uses: regime flip probabilities $p_{cs}=0.03$, $p_{sc}=0.08$ (Mixed preset; scaled $\times(0.35,1.40)$ Calm, $\times(2.00,0.50)$ Stormy); reversion targets Calm$(0.30,0.80,0.25)$ / Storm$(0.72,0.30,0.75)$; up to $K_{\max}=8$ storm cells with intensity decay $\tau \in \{0.006\,\text{storm}, 0.020\,\text{calm}\}$, radius $r = 8(1+0.15\sin\theta)\sqrt{I}$, and removal at $I<0.12$; an AR(1) background
%
\begin{equation}
  X_{t+1} = \rho_X\,\mathrm{adv}(X_t) + (1-\rho_X)\,\mu_X + \sigma_X(0.3+\mathfrak{u})\,\eta_t,
  \label{eq:ar1}
\end{equation}
%
with $(\rho_s,\rho_v,\rho_u)=(0.95,0.90,0.93)$, $(\sigma_s,\sigma_v,\sigma_u)=(0.07,0.06,0.07)$, unpredictability $\mathfrak{u}=0.30$, and $\eta \sim \mathcal{U}(-1,1)$; a system overlay $\omega = (1-\sqrt{d^2/r^2})^2 I$ adding to $s,u$ and subtracting $0.85\omega$ from $v$; cross-channel coupling $s \mathrel{+}= 0.35(u{-}0.5) - 0.20(v{-}0.5)$, $u \mathrel{-}= 0.25(v{-}0.5)$; and a 5-point smoother with gradient cap $G_{\max}=0.12$.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ==================================================================

The model's defensible core is the explicit, quantified coupling in Eq.~\ref{eq:pcomms}: a single collision-warning exchange succeeds only when both the environment and both crews permit it. This makes the central hypotheses (H1--H3) mechanistic rather than correlational --- fatigue management ($m$ in Eq.~\ref{eq:m}) acts on outcomes solely by changing how often warnings are delivered and how prepared crews are, with no other lever distinguishing the conditions. Re-baselining the report on the real Baltic--North Sea engine, with hand-authored tracks and manual ports, keeps the experiment controlled while remaining geographically realistic.

The accredited layers raise the model from an internal A/B comparison toward external validity. SIMCOL (Section~\ref{sec:consequence}) grounds casualty counts in collision mechanics rather than a tunable rate; IWRAP (Section~\ref{sec:iwrap}) places the resulting frequencies on a regulator-recognised scale; and the SAR subsystem (Section~\ref{sec:sar}) makes \texttt{survival\_ratio} and \texttt{avg\_tta\_hours} meaningful by modelling drift, random-search detection, and environmental degradation rather than instantaneous rescue. The SIMCOL external dynamics are pinned to the Pedersen--Zhang closed form, with the absorbed energy carried by the normal closing-velocity component and Lützen's added-mass coefficients (Eq.~\ref{eq:eabs}); the only modelling surrogate in the consequence chain is the survival factor $S_i$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:sfactor}), shaped to the SOLAS~II-1 $s$-factor because the engine does not resolve per-ship residual stability. The SAR environmental degradation is likewise pinned to Ashrafi's published month-group constants (Section~\ref{sec:sar-ashrafi}); the single remaining \texttt{TODO-VERIFY} marker is the optional force-field repulsion exponent (Section~\ref{sec:forcefield}), to be pinned against its source before final reporting.

Limitations follow from the controlled design. Hand-authored tracks trade empirical traffic realism for reproducibility; the deterministic lower-id give-way rule is a simplification of full COLREGs assignment; and the optional force-field track (Section~\ref{sec:forcefield}) is not the default. Calibration targets --- notably fewer than $0.5$ collisions per $1\,000$ ship-hours under Baseline~A in the Calm Passage scenario --- are grounded in AIS-derived near-miss encounter statistics~\cite{silveira2013,zhang2015}.
